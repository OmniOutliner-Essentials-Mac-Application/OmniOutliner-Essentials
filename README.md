## Six Moves From Blank Page To Working Agenda

**1. Write the outcome, not the title.** The very first row should say what the meeting has to produce —
"agree the launch date", not "launch meeting". Everything else in the document is going to hang beneath
that sentence, so it is worth twenty seconds of thought.

**2. Put the topics one level under it.** Type them as they occur to you and let the indent do the filing;
a row nudged inward becomes a subpoint of whatever sits above it, and a row nudged back out is promoted
again. Order does not matter yet, because reordering costs nothing here.

**3. Give every topic a number.** Add a column for minutes and fill it in honestly. The total appearing
next to a sixty-minute booking is usually the moment an agenda gets shorter, and it is the clearest example
of why extra columns beat writing "10 mins" into the middle of a sentence.

**4. Add owners as a popup list.** Names repeat, and typed-out names repeat inconsistently. A customizable
popup list holding the four people who ever lead a topic keeps the column tidy and turns filtering later
into a one-second job.

**5. Switch on tick boxes for decisions.** Smart checkboxes turn discussion points into things with a
finished state. During the meeting you tick as you go, and what is left unticked at the end is the follow-up
list, already written.

**6. Save the look once.** Decide how top-level rows and their children should appear, hand that to the
styles system, and every future agenda inherits it. This is the step people skip and then regret, because
without it each new file is quietly redesigned from scratch.

## After The Room Empties

The document is more useful in the twenty minutes after the meeting than during it. Collapse the topics
that closed so only live items remain visible. Duplicate the file for next month, clear the tick boxes, and
keep the timings — an agenda that has been through two rounds of reality estimates far better than a fresh
one. If actions came out of it, indent them under the topic that produced them rather than starting a
separate list, so the reason for each task stays attached to the task.

## Small Rules For Documents That Grow

Keep one file per recurring commitment. A standing team meeting, a personal to-do list and an expense record
each want their own window; merging them produces a file nobody opens. Let the top level stay short, because
fourteen main headings almost always means several of them belong a level down. Put measurable things in
columns and meaning in the row text, which keeps sorting from scrambling the sense of a section.

Two more habits pay for themselves. Re-read the top level alone, with everything folded, once a week — if it
does not make sense at that zoom, the structure is wrong rather than the content. And revisit the styles
whenever a file starts feeling heavy: uniform formatting makes every row look equally urgent, which is the
usual reason a long outline stops being read. That is the whole trick behind the omnioutliner essentials mac
download, and it works the same on an agenda, a screenplay draft or an event plan.
